# How to Write a Conference or Journal Abstract

A section-by-section reference for this project’s observational and ecological abstracts, and for clinical vignettes when a venue accepts them. It does not replace the target journal’s or conference’s instructions.

**Project standard (Jake, 2026-11-27):** Conference abstracts are **≤500 words including section labels**, unless the venue is stricter. Check whether the title, authors, headings, tables, or references count. Do not submit; Jake submits.

**Last updated:** 2026-11-27. Sources were verified on this date (see [Sources](#sources)).

---

## 1. Purpose, and what reviewers score

ICMJE notes that the abstract is often the only substantive part of an article that is indexed, and the only part many readers ever read. It must accurately reflect the work and must not overinterpret the findings.

Reviewers commonly score:

- **Importance / relevance** to the audience (general internal medicine, family medicine, otolaryngology).
- **Methods:** named design, clear sample, measures, confounders, and analysis.
- **Results-to-conclusions fit:** conclusions justified by the data shown; equal weight on positive and negative findings.
- **Originality** of the question or approach.
- **Writing quality:** clear and organized.

These domains appear in the SGIM scientific-abstract rubric, AAFP FUTURE first-round criteria, JAMA and AAO-HNSF conclusion rules, and ACP chapter poster sheets (Maryland ACP 2024). SGIM vignette review scores the clinical problem, a practice pearl, relevant literature, and writing quality. No published AAO-HNSF reviewer score sheet was found; the Academy still requires conclusions supported by the results.

## 2. Title

The title should be a distilled description that, with the abstract, makes electronic retrieval sensitive and specific (ICMJE). STROBE item 1(a) requires a commonly used design term (“cross-sectional,” “cohort,” “case-control”) in the title or the abstract. For routinely collected data, RECORD adds the data type and, when possible, the database name (1.1); region and time frame (1.2); and any linkage (1.3). Name the population, contrast, primary outcome, and design. Avoid abbreviations in the title (ICMJE). Honor venue length rules (AAO-HNSF scientific titles: 15 words).

## 3. Section-by-section (research abstracts)

Use the headings the venue requires. Common patterns:

- IMRAD: Background/Introduction, Methods, Results, Conclusions.
- JAMA Network original data (≤350 words): Importance; Objective; Design; Setting; Participants; Exposure(s); Main Outcome(s) and Measure(s); Results; Conclusions and Relevance.
- *Annals of Internal Medicine* (≤275 words): Background, Objective, Design, Setting, Patients or Participants, Interventions if any, Measurements, Results, Limitations, Conclusion, Primary Funding Source.

NLM/PubMed displays structured labels in uppercase followed by a colon.

Word-share ranges below are a **practical allocation, not an official standard**. They follow Andrade: Background shortest, Methods usually second-longest, Results longest. Scale them to a 250–500-word abstract.

### Background / Introduction — about 10–15% (2–3 sentences)

**Include:** What is known; the gap; why this setting matters. If there is no Objective heading, end with the purpose or a priori hypothesis (ACP).

**Common mistakes:** A long literature review that steals space from Results (Andrade). Vague openers (“X is an important problem”) with no gap.

### Objective / Aim / Hypothesis — about 5% if separate

**Include:** The precise question (JAMA: “To determine whether…”). State the main aim first. State a prespecified hypothesis when one was tested (JAMA; STROBE item 3).

**Common mistakes:** A list of aims with no primary question. A hypothesis that does not match the analysis.

### Methods — about 25–30%

**Include:** Design; setting; who was selected and how (eligibility, data source, codes or algorithms); time frame; exposures and primary outcomes; and the analysis, including confounding (ICMJE). RECORD expects the database or data type, region, and time frame in the title or abstract. In this project, name the Patient Cohort Explorer or the public sources, the measure (for example, ICD-10 H90.3), and whether county analyses are ecological.

**Common mistakes:** No design, N, dates, or outcome definition. Describing Otolaryngology contact as an access outcome when it is only the diagnostic setting.

### Results — about 40–50% (the longest section)

**Include:** Analyzed N and key characteristics first (JAMA). Then the primary result, and only essential secondary results, with denominators, effect sizes, and 95% CIs. It is not enough to say that an exposure is or is not “significantly associated” with an outcome (STROBE item 1(b) explanation). Never report a P value alone; place it after the numbers and the interval estimate (JAMA).

**Common mistakes:** “Data will be presented” or “results are pending.” AAFP FUTURE will not consider incomplete projects; ACP requires that conclusions be supported by data shown in the abstract. Qualitative claims without numbers (Andrade). Relative changes without underlying rates (JAMA). Findings that are not in the paper (ICMJE, STROBE, CONSORT for Abstracts).

### Conclusions — about 10–15% (a few precise sentences)

**Include:** The primary take-home message, any additional finding that changes interpretation, and a cautious implication (Andrade; JAMA). Note important limitations (ICMJE; *Annals* has a Limitations heading). Give equal emphasis to positive and negative findings of equal merit (JAMA; AAO-HNSF).

**Common mistakes:** Causal language from observational data. Practice or policy claims the data cannot support. “This is the first report of…” without a documented search (ACP vignette guide).

## 4. Clinical vignettes and case abstracts

ACP and SGIM accept clinical vignettes as teaching cases, not research reports.

**ACP format:** Title; a short Introduction (context and why the case matters; starting with the case is acceptable); a chronological Case Description (history, examination, investigations, course and outcome); and a Discussion that extracts one or a few teaching points. ACP National allows 500 words in the body; title, authors, and references are excluded; the portal is text only. Use at most about three abbreviations, spelled out at first use.

**CARE item 3** (journal case reports): Introduction — what the case adds; Case presentation — main symptoms, findings, diagnoses and interventions, and outcomes; Conclusion — the main take-away lessons.

Do not submit a case that turns on a trivial variation of a known entity, and do not claim “first case” lightly (ACP).

## 5. Reporting numbers

- Give exact counts and denominators, not only percentages.
- Give effect sizes with 95% CIs (RR, RD, OR, rho, as appropriate). Add the P value after the estimate, not instead of it.
- Lead with the analyzed sample. If people were excluded, ACP asks for the reason when space allows.
- Never write “data will be presented.” If a result is not yet obtained, leave it out of a submitted abstract; use `[PENDING]` only in internal drafts.
- Do not invent or back-calculate numbers. UMMC RDW cells under 11 are written `"<11 (suppressed)"`.
- The abstract must match the paper or poster. ICMJE and CONSORT for Abstracts both warn that abstracts often disagree with the full text.

## 6. Observational and ecological designs

Write **association**, not cause. STROBE covers cohort, case-control, and cross-sectional studies. Its authors noted that a formal ecological-studies extension would be welcome and does not yet exist, so apply STROBE’s abstract advice (name the design; give a balanced numerical summary) and state the unit of analysis.

For county- or area-level analyses, say the findings are ecological. County associations do not license inferences about individual patients (the ecological fallacy; Morgenstern 1995). Paper 01 already models this: county estimates “were ecological, not confirmatory.”

For routinely collected EHR or registry data, follow RECORD in the title or abstract: data type and database, region and time frame, and any linkage. Do not describe an unadjusted contrast as age-adjusted, or a cross-sectional snapshot as change over time.

## 7. Style

- **Tense.** Past tense for methods and results of completed work, as in ICMJE, JAMA, STROBE, and Andrade examples. Established background facts may be present tense. Conclusions may use present tense for what the results show, without turning them into universal laws.
- **Abbreviations.** Use only standard abbreviations; spell the term out at first use unless it is a standard unit (ICMJE). ACP recommends no more than three. Avoid them in the title.
- **References, tables, and figures.** Andrade treats citations in an abstract as almost invariably inappropriate. Some venues allow a short reference list (ACP National excludes references from the word count). Use a table only if the rules allow it; ACP’s national portal is text only.
- **Funding and registration.** ICMJE: list funding separately after the abstract; add a trial registration or public data-set identifier at the end when applicable.

## 8. Conference- and journal-specific notes

Limits change. Recheck the official page: word versus character count; whether spaces, headings, titles, and authors count; format; tables or references; membership and first-author eligibility; and the deadline and time zone.

| Venue or rule | What to verify |
|---|---|
| This project | ≤500 words including section labels |
| ACP National Abstract Competition | 500-word body; title, authors, and references excluded; text only; first author must be an ACP member |
| AAO-HNSF scientific oral or poster | 1,800 characters for Introduction, Methods, Results, and Conclusions combined; 15-word title |
| AAFP FUTURE posters | Headings include Project description, Objectives/purpose, Methodology, Applicant’s role, Results, Conclusions/discussion; the project must be complete; AAFP student or resident membership required |
| JAMA Network original-data abstracts | ≤350 words; specified headings (§3) |
| *Annals of Internal Medicine* | ≤275 words; includes Limitations |
| Many journals (Andrade) | Often 200–250 words |

## 9. Pre-submission checklist

- [ ] Current official instructions followed exactly.
- [ ] I know what counts toward the limit. The draft is under the venue cap and, for this project, ≤500 words including labels unless the venue is tighter.
- [ ] The title or abstract names the design (STROBE). Routinely collected sources, region, and time frame are identified (RECORD).
- [ ] Background states the gap; the objective or hypothesis is explicit.
- [ ] Methods name the design, setting, selection, dates, measures, and analysis.
- [ ] Results lead with N and report the primary contrast with denominators, effect size, and 95% CI, not a P value alone.
- [ ] No “data will be presented,” no `[PENDING]` results, no unsourced numbers, no UMMC cells under 11.
- [ ] Conclusions follow the results, use association rather than cause, note key limits, and (if ecological) do not infer individual-level effects from area-level data.
- [ ] Every number and claim also appears in the paper or poster, with the same values.
- [ ] Abbreviations are defined; no title abbreviations; no references or tables unless allowed.
- [ ] A mentor or co-author has read a draft aloud for clarity (ACP). Jake, not an agent, will submit.

## 10. Annotated skeleton

```
Title: [Population, contrast, outcome, design, place]. Avoid abbreviations.

Background: [1 sentence: what is known.] [1 sentence: the gap in this
population or setting.]

Objective: To [determine whether / estimate] [primary contrast] among
[population].

Methods: [Design] of [data type and named source], [place], [dates].
Eligible [patients/counties] were [criteria]. The primary outcome was
[definition]. We estimated [effect measure] with 95% CIs [and any
stratification or adjustment]. County analyses were ecological
[if applicable].

Results: Among [N] [units], [key composition]. [Primary result:
n/N (%) vs n/N (%); RR/RD x.xx, 95% CI x.xx–x.xx; P = .xx].
[One essential secondary result, similarly quantified.]

Conclusions: [Association in one sentence.] [Limitation or non-causal
caveat in one sentence.] [Cautious implication, if supported.]
```

## 11. Weak versus stronger sentences (Paper 01 numbers)

Examples use only figures already recorded in `papers/01-hearing-loss-disparities/draft-v2.md`. Illustrative, not a replacement abstract.

**Background**

- Weak: “Hearing loss is an important problem and disparities exist.”
- Stronger: “Hearing loss is common, yet disparities in diagnosis and specialty care persist by race and geography. Mississippi has a large rural population and a high disability burden, but local clinical data on hearing-loss care are limited.”

**Methods**

- Weak: “We studied hearing-loss patients at UMMC and looked at their visits.”
- Stronger: “We analyzed de-identified UMMC Patient Cohort Explorer counts for ICD-10 H90.3 (bilateral sensorineural hearing loss). The primary hypothesis was a lower rate of ≥1 Audiology-department encounter among Black than White patients after age stratification (Mantel-Haenszel RR). County data (CDC PLACES 2025, ACS 2020–2024, County Health Rankings 2025) were ecological, not confirmatory.”

**Results**

- Weak: “Black patients were significantly less likely to be seen in audiology (P < 0.05).”
- Stronger: “1,730/15,612 (11.08%) had ≥1 Audiology encounter, less often among Black than White patients (630/6,176, 10.20% vs 996/8,597, 11.59%; RR 0.880, 95% CI 0.801–0.968; P = 0.008). The Mantel-Haenszel RR was 0.812 (95% CI 0.711–0.928) after age stratification.”

**Conclusions**

- Weak: “Black patients are denied audiology care, so health systems must immediately expand access.”
- Stronger: “Audiology visits were uncommon and modestly less frequent among Black than White patients; the gap persisted after age and sex stratification.” Then state the actual limits (single center, one diagnosis code, no age-adjusted prevalence, ecological county context only) rather than a causal or policy leap.
